# Reading Lists

## Philosophy

A reading list that says "read this paper" is not a reading list — it is a bibliography. The difference matters. A bibliography tells you what exists. A reading list tells you *why you should read it, what to focus on, and what you should be able to do after reading it.*

This reading program follows the MIT tradition of curated, purposeful reading. In 6.036, every reading assignment comes with specific focus questions — not "read Bishop Chapter 3" but "read Bishop §3.1–3.3, focus on the bias-variance derivation, and be prepared to derive the bias-variance decomposition from memory." In 6.5950, every reading comes with a specific connection to the lab — not "read the Sponge paper" but "read §4 of the Sponge paper, focus on how cache-set conflicts create observation channels, and be prepared to trace a concrete cache-timing attack in the next lab."

We apply the same standard here.

## Structure

Each reading entry contains five elements:

1. **Full Citation** — Enough information to find the source. DOI or URL when available.
2. **Why You Should Read It** — One paragraph explaining the relevance to this course. This is not a summary; it is a motivation.
3. **Focus Sections** — Specific sections, chapters, or pages. Not "the whole thing." You will not read every word of every reading, and that is intentional. The focus sections are the ones that matter for the control-theoretic framework.
4. **Key Questions** — Two to four questions you should be able to answer after reading. These are the questions that will appear on problem sets and in class discussions. If you cannot answer them, re-read.
5. **Connection to Control Theory** — One paragraph explaining how the reading connects to the control-loop model that underpins this course. This is the bridge between the reading and the framework.

## Reading Schedule

Readings are assigned per phase, one phase per week. Each reading list is designed to take 3–5 hours total. The focus sections are calibrated to this time budget.

## How to Read

1. **Read with the key questions in front of you.** You are not reading for general knowledge; you are reading to answer specific questions. This focuses your attention and prevents the "I read it but can't remember the key point" problem.

2. **Annotate with control-loop vocabulary.** Every time you encounter a security concept, map it to a control-loop element: Is this an observation failure? A controller compromise? An unsafe actuation? A feedback manipulation? Over time, this mapping becomes automatic, and that is the point.

3. **Read the focus sections twice, skim the rest once.** The focus sections contain the arguments and evidence you need. The rest provides context. Do not spend equal time on all sections.

4. **Discuss before class.** Form a reading group of 2–3 people. Meet for 30 minutes before each class and compare answers to the key questions. Disagreements are the most valuable outcome — they reveal assumptions you did not know you were making.
